01

What the DAM is

Screendragon's DAM is a home for a brand's digital assets — one place to store, find, control and share the images, videos, documents and templates a team works with every day. It does the things you'd expect of a serious asset library: fast search across everything, structured tagging (with AI help), and a clean way to hand assets out. What sets it apart is governance: the DAM knows what each asset is cleared for and enforces it — the right people get the right version for the right market, and the wrong asset doesn't slip into the wrong place.

Put simply, it's built for teams who can't afford to use an out-of-date logo, an expired image, or an asset that was never cleared for their region. Rights, versions and approvals aren't bolted on — they're the point.

The elevator pitch A brand asset library that doesn't just store files — it keeps them on-brand, in-rights and up to date. Teams find the right asset in seconds, always get a version they're allowed to use, and share it safely inside or outside the business, with every share tracked.
Where it's heading — worth flagging The DAM is designed to plug into Screendragon's Unity workflow platform, so an asset stays connected to the work that produced and approved it. That integration is our biggest long-term differentiator, but it isn't fully built or live yet — it lands in a future release. Before positioning it to a client as available today, check with the Product team for current status.
02

What makes ours different

Most of what a DAM does — storage, search, tags — every product has. Those things are expected; they don't set anything apart. Three things make ours different. Two are live and working today; the third is where our Unity integration will take it, and is flagged accordingly.

Rights that actually stop misuse

A basic library controls an asset up to the moment it's downloaded, then it's blind. Ours governs the asset itself: rights, territories, channels and expiry are attached to the file, and the DAM enforces them — it hands out the version you're allowed and holds back the ones you're not. Enforcement isn't a label on the screen; it's a real gate the file has to pass. Live today.

AI tagging you can trust

Auto-tagging itself is ordinary — everyone has it. What's ours is that every tag remembers where it came from: machine-suggested or human-added, and both if both. You can see it, confirm it, filter by it, or remove it in bulk. The value isn't the tags; it's that machine-made metadata stays governable. Live today.

Worth being precise about: today the AI tags come from a generic image-recognition service (AWS Rekognition) — good general labels, but it can't yet be trained on your brand's own taxonomy or tuned by an agent. Making it brand-trainable is planned but not yet scoped; check with the Product team before positioning it.

History that travels with the file Unity — coming

The bigger prize: when an asset comes out of an approved Screendragon workflow, it will carry a frozen record of what was approved, by whom, and when — history that stays attached for the asset's whole life, even if the original job is deleted. A pure-play DAM has no workflow to inherit this from. This depends on the Unity integration, which isn't live yet — check with the Product team before positioning it as available.

One thing to keep in mind This guide describes how the product is designed to work — not everything is finished today, and the deepest workflow features depend on the Unity integration, which is still being built. Before promising any of it to a customer or partner, check with the Product team for what's live right now.

04

The things you'll actually do

Almost all DAM use comes down to a handful of everyday tasks. Learn these and you can work in the product.

1
Find an asset

Search from the top bar for anything — a filename, a tag, words inside a document or image. Then narrow the results with filters down the left: file type, rights state, date, who uploaded it.

2
Upload an asset

Drag files in. They start uploading immediately while you add tags and details. The DAM can read structure straight from a filename and suggest tags automatically — so good metadata lands without much typing.

3
Edit an asset

Change an asset's details or rights after upload — one file at a time, or many at once. Select a batch and the same change applies across all of them.

4
Share an asset

Share inside the platform with a colleague, or send an external link to someone with no account. External links are locked down by default: they expire, need email verification, and don't allow download unless you switch it on.

5
Download the right version

Download gives you the best version your rights allow. Download as opens a picker for a specific size, format or channel crop — and only offers crops the asset is actually licensed for.

6
Check an asset's rights & status

Every asset shows its state on the card and in the full view: is it live, expired, embargoed; which regions and channels it's cleared for; who owns it. If an action isn't allowed, the product tells you why rather than hiding it.

Here's each one on screen.

4.1  Finding an asset — the library

Search is smarter than a filename match. Two things worth knowing about what it can find:

Finds text inside files (OCR)

The DAM reads the words inside images and documents and indexes them, so a search can match text that lives in an asset — not just its filename or tags. When a result matches this way, it's flagged as matching extracted text, and the matched term is highlighted so you can see why it came up.

Smart, AI-assisted search

One search covers everything at once — metadata, attributes, tags and the text found inside files. And from any asset's lightbox, Similar finds visually related assets by content, not keywords, each with a match score. So you can search by what an asset says, how it's labelled, or what it looks like.

Two kinds of label — and why it matters when you search.

Attributes are structured fields your admin sets up, with fixed values to choose from — things like Country, Orientation or Product Level. They're consistent because everyone picks from the same list.

Tags are free-form keywords. People add them, and the DAM's AI adds them too. That's the part worth knowing: a small marker on a tag tells you it was AI-suggested rather than added by a person — so you can see where a label came from and decide whether to trust it, keep it, or filter it out. There's no separate "AI" filter and no AI section; AI is simply a property of the tag it produced.

The AI tags currently come from a generic image-recognition service (AWS Rekognition) — general labels, not yet trainable on your own brand taxonomy. Brand-trainable tagging is planned but unscoped.

4.2  Previewing an asset — Lightbox

Click any asset and it opens in the lightbox — a full-screen viewer that steps back so the asset itself is the hero. Everything about that asset lives in one pull-out rail on the right, organised into five tabs. You never leave the viewer to see an asset's details, rights, or related files.

Details — the file facts: filename, dimensions, size, who uploaded it and when, plus a read-only File metadata section (embedded EXIF/IPTC, when present).
Attributes — the asset's structured attributes and its Tags. AI-suggested tags carry a small marker on the chip, so you can always tell machine-added tags from human ones.
Similar — visually related assets, each with a match score (84%, 83%…). An asset's rights state travels here too — a restricted result still shows its Expired or Embargoed pill.
Rights — the asset's rights, read-only, in three groups: time-based (embargo, expiry), licensing (licence type, owner, documentation), and permitted use (territories, channels, audiences). This is where you check what an asset is cleared for before you use it.
Download — the same version-and-format picker as Download as, right inside the rail: choose a version tier and one or more channel formats without opening a separate dialog.

4.4  Editing an asset

Once an asset is in the library, you edit it on a dedicated surface. How much you can change depends on whether you're editing one asset or several at once — and the surface adapts to match.

Editing a single asset gives you the full set of controls, down the left as tabs: Details, Attributes, Adjustments, Template, Versions, Rights and Access. The asset fills the canvas so you can see what you're working on.

Editing several at once — bulk edit — narrows to just the two panels that make sense to apply across a whole set: Attributes and Rights. Tick the files you want, change a field once, and it lands on all of them.

One asset vs many — the difference in a line Editing a single asset gives you everything, including Versions (upload a replacement, keep the link and history). Editing many at once narrows to Attributes and Rights — the fields it makes sense to apply in bulk. Same editor, scoped to what you're doing.

Reading an asset card

The asset card is the thing you'll see most and, at first, understand least. It packs an asset's whole state into a few small marks. Here's the anatomy — this wireframe I've built from the design system, so it's accurate now.

The pills you'll meet most, live:

Active
Live and usable.
Expired
Rights have lapsed. Download is blocked; a request routes to the owner as a renewal.
Embargoed
Not released yet. Held until its date or an explicit grant.
Draft
Saved but not published to the library yet.
US-only
Region or scope limit — where the asset is cleared for use.
JPG MP4 PDF
File-type badges — a plain grey marker showing the format.
Why nothing is hidden If you can't do something — download a restricted file, share an unapproved one — the control stays visible but disabled, with a note explaining the condition. The product never makes a control vanish, because a missing button tells you nothing: you can't tell "not allowed" from "broken." A disabled one with a reason answers both.
06

The dashboard

The dashboard is your landing view — the first thing you see, and a fast way back into everything. It's built entirely from widgets: self-contained blocks like an event banner, a folder browser, collections, and recent activity. What makes it more than a home page is that it resolves by scope — the same dashboard shows different things depending on whether you're looking across everything or through the lens of one folder.

6.1  Across all folders

With the scope set to All folders, the dashboard gives you the big picture: a banner for the active event, a browser for every folder you can see, and the collections shared with you.

6.2  Through one folder

Pick a single folder from that same dropdown and the dashboard recomposes around it — now the widgets show this folder's collections, its most recent assets, and your recent activity within it. Same framework, focused on one event.

Who arranges it The dashboard is assembled from widgets, and an admin decides which widgets appear and in what order for the whole DAM (in Settings). It's a configured layout rather than something each person rearranges — so everyone gets a consistent starting view, and the scope switch is how you focus it.
07

Other areas

Beyond the library, the lightbox and the dashboard, a few other areas do specific jobs. This section covers them one at a time.

7.1  Collections

A collection is a hand-picked set of assets you assemble for a purpose — a campaign, a pitch, a hand-off. Unlike a folder (which is where an asset lives and who can see it), a collection can pull assets from anywhere, and an asset can sit in many collections at once. Collections are also the thing you share — internally or as an external link.

Folders vs Collections — worth keeping straight A folder is where an asset lives and who's allowed to see it — structure and permission. A collection is a set you assemble for a purpose, drawing from any folders, and it's what you share out. One asset lives in a single folder but can appear in many collections.

7.2  Access approvals

When someone hits an asset they're not cleared to reach, they can request access rather than hitting a dead end. Those requests land here, in a queue an approver works through. Every request carries who asked, why, and when — and every decision is recorded.

This is about approving access to a file — letting someone through to an asset they couldn't otherwise reach. It's not the same as approving the asset's content (signing a design off), which is a separate thing.

7.3  Downloads

The Downloads area is your record of everything you've pulled out of the DAM, split into two views. Download Queue is where downloads are staged and configured before they run — the place to set versions and formats on a batch. Download History is the log of everything you've already downloaded, so you can pull any of it again.

7.4  Shared links

Every external link you've created, in one place. Where a collection's own details panel (see 7.1) shows the links for that collection, this is the view across everything — one table to see, audit, and shut down any link you've shared out.

7.5  Settings

Settings is the admin area that shapes how the DAM behaves — what's turned on, how rights work, how uploads are handled, what the dashboard shows. Most people never need it, but it's worth knowing two things about how it works.

Settings apply at two levels. Most options are set once for the whole DAM, and then can be overridden for an individual folder where that folder needs to behave differently. So a rule can be global by default but tuned per folder — the same setting, two levels of control.

Two levels, one idea A setting like "collections are enabled" or "uploads require rights" is normally set once for the whole DAM. Where a particular folder needs to differ, an admin can override that setting for that folder alone. Everywhere else keeps the global value. You rarely need to think about this unless you're an admin — but it's why one folder can behave slightly differently from the rest.
